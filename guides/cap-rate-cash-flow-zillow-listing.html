How to run a quick cap rate and cash flow check on a Zillow listing

Screen a Zillow listing as a rental in five minutes: rent from comps, NOI, then cap rate, cash flow, DSCR and cash-on-cash. Worked example.

For investors7 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: estimate the rent from comps, subtract vacancy and operating costs to get net operating income (NOI), divide NOI by the price for the cap rate, and subtract the annual loan payment for cash flow. Five inputs and a calculator get you a screen in minutes. Here’s the order, a worked example, and the mistakes that flip the answer.

The four numbers

  • NOI (net operating income): rent collected minus operating costs, before the loan.
  • Cap rate: NOI ÷ purchase price. What the property earns as a percentage of its price if you paid cash.
  • Cash flow: NOI minus annual debt service (your loan payments). What’s left in your pocket.
  • DSCR (debt service coverage ratio): NOI ÷ annual debt service. Lenders on investor loans typically look for roughly 1.2 or better; below 1.0 the rent doesn’t cover the mortgage.

Cash-on-cash return is cash flow divided by the cash you put in (down payment plus closing costs). It’s the number that tells you whether the money is working.

Step 1: Rent

Pull rental comps and use the median asking rent for the unit, converted to effective rent if concessions are common. For a multifamily building, estimate each unit type separately and add them up. If the listing reports current rents, use those for the as-is case and the comp median for the upside case.

Step 2: Operating costs

  • Vacancy: 5–8 percent of gross rent is a common screening assumption.
  • Property taxes: start from the listing’s tax history, then correct it. Many states reassess at sale. In California the new bill is roughly 1.1–1.25 percent of your purchase price, not the seller’s old bill. In Texas, effective rates are often around 2 percent. When in doubt, use your state’s typical effective rate on the asking price. How to recompute the tax from your price and estimate insurance walks through it. Estimate your tax after reassessment.
  • Insurance: a state average is fine for a screen; get a quote before you write an offer, especially in wind, wildfire or flood areas.
  • Maintenance and repairs: 5–10 percent of rent, higher for older buildings.
  • Management: 8–10 percent of rent if you’ll hire it out. Include it even if you self-manage; your time isn’t free.
  • HOA dues, utilities you pay, landscaping, trash: whatever the listing or the HOA says.

Step 3: The loan

Down payment, rate and term. For a quick monthly principal-and-interest figure, any mortgage calculator works. Investor loans usually carry a higher rate and a bigger down payment than owner-occupied loans, so don’t screen with the rate you saw advertised for a primary residence.

A worked example (sample numbers)

Say a house is listed at $500,000 and similar 3-beds nearby are asking $2,800 a month.

  • Gross rent: $2,800 × 12 = $33,600
  • Vacancy at 5%: −$1,680 → $31,920
  • Taxes (1.1% of price): −$5,500
  • Insurance: −$1,500
  • Maintenance at 8%: −$2,688
  • Management at 8%: −$2,688
  • NOI: $19,544
  • Cap rate: $19,544 ÷ $500,000 = 3.9%

Now the loan: 25% down ($125,000), $375,000 financed at 6.75% over 30 years is about $2,432 a month, or $29,187 a year.

  • Cash flow: $19,544 − $29,187 = −$9,643 a year (about −$800 a month)
  • DSCR: $19,544 ÷ $29,187 = 0.67

That’s a listing that doesn’t work as a rental at this price with this loan, and you found out in five minutes. To get to break-even you’d need rent closer to $3,800, a much lower price, or a lot more cash down. Run the same arithmetic on three or four candidates and the one that clears is obvious.

A cap rate is only comparable when everyone builds NOI the same way. If a listing brochure quotes a cap rate, ask whether it includes vacancy, management and a reassessed tax bill. Many don’t, and the real number is a point or two lower.

The mistakes that flip the answer

  • Using the seller’s tax bill in a state that reassesses at sale.
  • Using the Rent Zestimate for a whole building, or for a unit where comps say otherwise.
  • Forgetting HOA dues on condos and townhomes. They can erase the cash flow by themselves.
  • Skipping management because you’ll self-manage, then selling to a buyer who won’t.
  • Counting appreciation. It may come, but it isn’t cash flow and it won’t pay the mortgage this year.
