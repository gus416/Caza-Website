Will house hacking a duplex cash flow? How to check before you buy

How to test a duplex house hack on a Zillow listing: rent for the other unit from rental comps, the full monthly cost, and a live-in and fully rented worked example.

Investor guide5 min readUpdated November 2026

Short answer: Often not at rates around 7%. Estimate the other unit’s rent from active rental comps, add every monthly cost, then test two cases: you live in one unit, and both units rented. In the sample below, both cases lose money, and the numbers show by how much.

How much rent will the other unit bring in?

Start with nearby active rentals of the same type, bedroom count and condition. Take the median asking rent, not the highest. Then adjust it:

  • Effective rent: if listings offer weeks free, spread the discount over the lease to get what a tenant really pays.
  • Vacancy: hold back a share of rent for empty months. The example uses 5%.
  • Stale listings: a rental that sat for months is asking too much. Ignore it.

The matching rules are in how to estimate rent with rental comps. Asking rents are not signed leases. If the listing reports current rents, compare them with the comp median. A tenant already in place may pay less than the market, and you may not be able to raise the rent until the lease ends.

What does a duplex cost each month?

Count every line, not just the mortgage:

  • Principal and interest on your loan, plus mortgage insurance if your down payment is small.
  • Property tax after reassessment. The tax on the listing is often the seller’s bill. Recompute it from your price: how to estimate property tax and insurance.
  • Insurance for a two-unit building.
  • Maintenance reserve for repairs on a building that now has a tenant.
  • Utilities you pay, which in a duplex often include shared meters.

Will the duplex cash flow? A worked example

Say a duplex is listed at $700,000, with two 2-bed units. Rental comps give a median of $2,300 for a 2-bed. You put 5% down on an owner-occupied loan at an illustrative 7% for 30 years. Other inputs are assumptions: property tax at 1.25% of price, insurance $200 a month, mortgage insurance at 0.6% of the loan a year, maintenance reserve $350, utilities $250, vacancy 5%.

Live-in case. After the other unit’s effective rent, you pay $4,101 a month to live there. If you rent a similar 2-bed today for $2,600, that is $1,501 more than you pay now, though part of the payment builds equity.

Fully rented case. Operating costs without the loan are $1,529 a month, so NOI is $34,090 a year. On $700,000 that is a 4.9% cap rate. Debt service on the loan is $53,091 a year in principal and interest, so DSCR is about 0.64 and cash flow is about -$22,991 a year on $35,000 down. A cap rate that low against a loan costing about 7.98% a year is negative leverage; this guide explains why.

With 3.5% down instead, the loan is $675,500, principal and interest is $4,494 and mortgage insurance is $338, so the live-in cost rises to $4,176 a month.

What would have to change?

To break even fully rented, the two units would need about $6,617 a month in gross rent, roughly $3,308 each, against $2,300 from the comps. That gap says the price, the rate or the down payment has to move, not the rent estimate. Try a lower price, a larger down payment, or a rate you can buy down. Also test the unit you live in: if you moved out, would it rent for the comp median?

Occupancy rules, minimum down payments and how much of the other unit’s rent a lender counts all vary. Owner-occupied loan rules differ by program and lender, so check with a lender before you rely on any of this.

What should you run before making an offer?

  1. Pull the median rent for each unit type from active rental comps.
  2. Recompute property tax and insurance from your own price and location.
  3. Fill in the live-in and fully rented columns with your own loan terms.
  4. Compare the live-in cost with what you pay in rent today, and the cap rate with the loan constant.
  5. Ask a lender what your program requires and how it treats the other unit’s rent.

FAQ

Can you make money house hacking a duplex?

Sometimes, but not always. In the sample here, a $700,000 duplex at 5% down and an illustrative 7% rate costs $4,101 a month to live in after the other unit pays $2,185. Check the math on each listing.

How do you estimate the other unit's rent?

Use the median asking rent from nearby active rentals of the same bedroom count, adjust for concessions and vacancy, and ignore stale listings. Asking rents are not signed leases.

What costs do people forget when house hacking?

Property tax after reassessment on your purchase price, mortgage insurance on a small down payment, a maintenance reserve, vacancy, and utilities you pay on shared meters.

Do owner-occupied loans have special rules?

Yes. Down payments, occupancy requirements and how much rental income a lender counts vary by program and lender. Confirm the current rules with a lender before you buy.
