The 50% rule for rental property: a quick screen, not a budget

What the rule of thumb counts as an expense, what it leaves out, how to screen a listing with it, and why the real tax bill, insurance quote and rent comps have to replace it.

For investors7 min readLast updated November 25, 2026

Short answer

The 50% rule is an investor’s rule of thumb: assume a rental’s operating expenses will take about half of its gross rent, and that the other half is what’s left before the mortgage.2 Use it to decide which listings deserve a closer look. Don’t use it as a budget. Once a property gets past the screen, swap the 50% for real numbers.

  1. Halve the gross rent. That’s the rule’s guess at operating costs. The other half has to cover the mortgage.
  2. Compare that other half with the loan payment. If it falls short, the listing needs a strong reason to stay on your list.
  3. Replace the guess. Use the tax bill you would pay, an insurance quote, and rent from comparable rentals.

1. What is the 50% rule for rental property?

It’s a shortcut for the expense side of a rental. DealCheck calls it “a quick rule of thumb to estimate rental property expenses,” where operating expenses are “expenses that do not include the loan payments.”1 Tellus puts it this way: “The 50% rule says to assume that operating expenses, everything except the mortgage payment, will take half of that rent.”2

The arithmetic is one line: monthly rent × 12 ÷ 2. A home renting for $2,000 a month brings in $24,000 a year, so the rule guesses $12,000 of operating costs and $12,000 left before the mortgage.

Treat it as what it is. The sources we read present it as a practitioner habit, not a research finding. SmartAsset says it’s “meant to be a guideline rather than a carved-in-stone standard” and “a screening tool, not a final investment analysis.”3 DealCheck warns that its estimates “may be significantly lower or higher than the actual expenses.”1

It isn’t the 1% rule, which compares rent with price. The 50% rule ignores the price and splits the rent into costs and what’s left.

2. What does the 50% cover, and what does it leave out?

Practitioner sources don’t agree on the exact list. Tellus puts management (“even if you manage the property yourself”), HOA dues and savings for big replacements inside the half.2 SmartAsset says its version “doesn’t factor in mortgage payments, property management fees or HOA dues.”3 They don’t agree on the base either: DealCheck halves “operating income,” which it defines as gross rent plus other income minus a vacancy allowance, so vacancy comes out before the 50%, not inside it.1 This guide uses the broader version on gross rent, following Tellus. Whichever you pick, use it for every listing you compare.

Inside the 50%:

  • Vacancy and unpaid rent
  • Property taxes and insurance
  • Repairs and maintenance
  • Property management, even if you self-manage
  • Reserves for roofs, furnaces and appliances (capex)
  • Owner-paid utilities, HOA dues, lawn care, legal and accounting

Outside the 50%:

  • Mortgage principal and interest
  • Your income taxes

Those lists follow Tellus.2 One wrinkle: in the strict definition, capex reserves sit below net operating income (NOI). The National Apartment Association’s CAM course guide defines NOI as revenue left “after all operating expenses, but before mortgage debt service and capital expenditures (or replace reserve payments) are deducted,” and says operating expenses do “not include capital items, reserves or debt service.”4 So if your 50% includes a reserve, the half that’s left sits below strict NOI by the amount of that reserve. Keep the reserve as its own line when you do the real numbers, so you can see both.

3. How do you use the 50% rule as a first screen?

  1. Get a rent you trust from similar rentals nearby; see how to estimate rent with rental comps.
  2. Annualize and halve it. Monthly rent × 12 ÷ 2 is the rough figure left before the mortgage.
  3. Compare with the loan. Get the yearly principal and interest from a lender quote or a mortgage calculator, for the loan you would actually use.
  4. Decide what’s next. If half the rent doesn’t cover the payment, the deal would need a lower price, more cash down or a better rent. If it does, move on to the real numbers.

It works in reverse too. If a seller’s operating statement shows costs far below what the rule would guess, ask what’s missing (vacancy, management, reserves, a new owner’s tax bill) before you rely on it.

4. Why do real numbers have to replace it?

Because expenses aren’t set by the rent. As SmartAsset puts it, “Actual expenses can vary widely depending on location, property type and management costs.”3 Once a listing passes the screen, replace the guess line by line:

  • Property tax. The tax on a listing is often the seller’s bill, not yours after buying.5 See how to estimate property tax and insurance, and check with the county.
  • Insurance. Get a quote for this building and address, with rental coverage.
  • Rent. Use rental comps, not a single estimate.
  • Repairs and reserves. Base them on the age and condition of the roof, systems and appliances. An inspection beats any percentage.
  • Everything else. HOA dues, utilities you pay and management quotes.

5. What happens when two rentals have the same rent but different costs?

Hypothetical example. These two properties and every number below are made up to show the arithmetic. They aren’t typical, they aren’t benchmarks, and they aren’t from any real listing. Both rent for $2,500 a month, so the 50% rule gives them the same answer: $30,000 of gross rent, $15,000 of costs and $15,000 left before the mortgage.

Property A: the shortcut is too cautious. Real costs with the reserve are $11,600, leaving $18,400, so the rule’s $15,000 is $3,400 too low. A’s NOI before reserves ($19,900) is $4,900 above it.

Property B: the shortcut is too generous. Real costs are $21,600, leaving $8,400, so the rule’s $15,000 is $6,600 too high. Even B’s NOI before reserves ($10,800) is $4,200 below it.

Add a loan. Say each came with a hypothetical $1,000 a month of principal and interest, or $12,000 a year. The 50% rule says both clear it by $3,000 ($15,000 − $12,000). With real numbers, A clears it by $6,400 ($18,400 − $12,000) and B falls short by $3,600 ($8,400 − $12,000). The shortcut passed both; with these made-up numbers, only one covers the payment.

What this guide can’t tell you

  • What any specific property will cost to own and run
  • What share of rent expenses take in your area or for your property type
  • Your tax bill after a purchase, or what insurance will cost (get the county’s figure and a quote)
  • The rent a unit will actually lease for
  • Whether a property is a good investment for you, or what a lender will approve

FAQ

What is the 50% rule for rental property?

It's a rule of thumb used by investors: assume a rental's operating expenses will take about half of its gross rent, before any mortgage payment. It's a quick screen for deciding which listings deserve a closer look, not an estimate of what a specific property will cost to run.

Does the 50% rule include the mortgage payment?

No. Mortgage principal and interest are left out, and so are your income taxes. The other half of the rent is what's left to cover the loan.2

How is the 50% rule different from the 1% rule?

The 1% rule compares monthly rent with the purchase price. The 50% rule splits the rent between operating costs and what's left before the mortgage. Both are quick screens, and neither uses your real tax bill, insurance quote or repair needs.

Is 50% accurate for the property I'm looking at?

It might be close, or far off in either direction. Taxes, insurance, the building's age and condition, HOA dues and management all depend on the property and the place. Replace the 50% with real numbers before you make an offer.
