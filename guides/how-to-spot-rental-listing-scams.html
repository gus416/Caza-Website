How to spot a rental listing scam before you pay anything

The signs of a fake rental listing: rent far below similar listings nearby, a request to wire money or pay before a tour, a lister who cannot show the unit, and photos copied from another listing. What to check, and the FTC guidance to follow.

For renters5 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: a rental listing deserves a hard look when the rent is far below what similar units nearby are asking, when anyone asks for money before you have toured the unit and signed a lease, when the person listing it cannot or will not show it, or when the photos and text appear on another listing under a different name or price. The Federal Trade Commission’s advice: see the place before you sign or pay, in person, through someone you trust, or at least by video tour; be suspicious of rent well below the going rate; and never pay by wire transfer, gift card or cryptocurrency. We would add: no payment-app transfer to an individual you have not verified. The FTC’s guide, Rental Listing Scams on consumer.ftc.gov, is the one to read.

Scams work because renting is urgent: scammers copy a real listing, drop the rent, and collect deposits from people in a hurry. The defense is a short checklist you run before money moves.

The signs

1. The rent is far below similar listings

This is the most reliable signal, and the easiest to check. Pull up similar rentals nearby on Zillow, same bedrooms, same kind of building, and look at what they are asking. A listing at $1,200 when every comparable 2-bed within half a mile is asking $2,200 to $2,400 is not a lucky find. Either it has a problem the ad is not telling you, or it is not real. The rent comparison guide shows how to pull those comps in about fifteen minutes.

2. Money before a tour, or before a lease

A deposit to “hold” a unit you have not seen, an application fee sent by payment app to a personal account, first month’s rent to get the keys mailed: these are the scam itself. A legitimate landlord or manager shows the unit, runs an application and signs a lease before collecting a deposit, and gives you a receipt from the business.

3. The lister cannot show the unit

“I’m out of the country,” “my agent will mail the keys after you pay,” “just look through the window.” A real owner has a way to let you in, through a manager, an agent or a scheduled showing. If no one can open the door, do not send money for what is behind it.

4. Wire, gift card, crypto or payment app

Any request to wire money, buy gift cards, send cryptocurrency, or pay by Zelle, Venmo or Cash App to an individual is a red flag by itself. These payments are hard or impossible to reverse, which is exactly why scammers ask for them.

5. A copied listing

Scammers lift photos and text from a for-sale listing or a legitimate rental and repost them with a lower rent and their own contact details. Search the address on Zillow and in a search engine. If the same photos show up at a different price, under a different name or as a home for sale, you have found the original and the copy. A reverse image search on a photo works too.

6. Pressure and a sloppy process

“Three other people want it, send the deposit tonight.” A lease by text with no business name. No application or screening at all. A lister who will only text, never call. None of these is proof, but together they are a pattern.

What to check before paying anything

  1. Tour in person, or have someone you trust tour for you. Walk in through the front door, with the person who will sign the lease as the landlord or their agent.
  2. Verify who owns or manages the property. Most counties have an online property records search where you can look up the owner of record by address. If a management company is involved, call the number on its own website, not the number in the listing, and ask whether the unit is listed and at what rent.
  3. Search the address on Zillow and elsewhere for the same photos at a different price or under a different name.
  4. Get a written lease from a named business or owner before any deposit, and get a receipt. Pay in a way that leaves a record, such as a check or a card, to the business named on the lease.
  5. Never wire money, buy gift cards, or send crypto for a rental, and do not send a payment-app transfer to an individual you have not met and verified.
  6. Protect your identity. Do not send a photo of your ID, your Social Security number or a bank login to a lister you have not verified.

One rule covers almost every case: do not pay for a rental you have not seen, and do not pay someone you have not verified. The FTC says to look at the property before you sign or pay, in person, through someone you know, or by video. A legitimate landlord loses nothing by waiting for you to tour.

If you have already paid, or spotted one

Contact your bank or the payment service right away and ask to reverse or dispute the transfer. Report the listing on Zillow from the listing page so it comes down for the next person. Report the scam to the FTC at ReportFraud.ftc.gov, to your local police, and to your state attorney general. If your identity information was sent, the FTC’s IdentityTheft.gov walks through next steps. For questions about deposits and lease terms, check your state or city’s tenant resources.
