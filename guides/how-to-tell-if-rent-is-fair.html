Is this rent fair? How to compare an apartment’s rent with similar listings on Zillow

How to tell if an apartment's rent is in line: match bedrooms, bathrooms, size and building type on Zillow's rentals map, use active asking rents, convert free weeks to effective rent, and read where the listing lands.

For renters5 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: an asking rent is in line when it sits near the middle of what similar, currently listed rentals nearby are asking, once every listing is converted to its effective rent. “Similar” means the same number of bedrooms, close in bathrooms and square feet, the same kind of building, and close by. Six to ten matches are enough. If the rent is above most of them, you have a reason to negotiate or keep looking. If it is far below all of them, verify the listing before you pay anything.

Rent is set by what other landlords nearby can get for the same kind of unit this month, so the only honest way to answer “is this fair?” is to look at the same kind of unit, nearby, listed now. Here is the process on Zillow.

1. Pull similar listings from Zillow’s rentals map

On Zillow, switch the search to Rent and type the address of the apartment you are considering. Use the Draw tool to outline an area around it: about half a mile in a dense city, one to two miles where rentals are spread out. Then filter to the same number of bedrooms. Keep the property type the same too. An apartment in a building comps against apartments; a house against houses; a condo or townhome against those. A 2-bed in a 300-unit building and a 2-bed house with a yard are different products with different rents.

Collect six to ten active listings that match on:

  • Bedrooms: exactly the same. This is the strongest driver of rent.
  • Bathrooms: within half a bath.
  • Size: within about 15–20% of the square footage, when the listing shows it.
  • Building type and finish: the photos tell you quickly whether the kitchen is comparable.
  • What is included: parking, in-unit laundry, utilities. They move the number.

For each one, note the asking rent, square feet, days listed, any required monthly fees, and any concession such as “6 weeks free” or “$500 off.” Fewer than four matches and you are guessing; widen the area before you loosen the bedroom rule.

2. Use active asking rents, not past rents

Zillow shows what landlords are asking today, not what tenants signed for, so compare against active listings. The Rent Zestimate is a model’s guess and a fine sanity check, but it is a single number with nothing behind it; real listings beat it. Remember that asking rents are a ceiling: where listings sit for weeks and offer free months, signed leases land below the ask.

3. Convert every listing to effective rent

A concession changes the comparison. “$2,600 with six weeks free on a 12-month lease” is not a $2,600 apartment for the first year. Six weeks is about 1.4 months, so the effective rent is $2,600 × (12 − 1.4) ÷ 12, or roughly $2,300 a month. A flat “$500 off” is $500 ÷ 12, about $42 a month, off the rent. Do this for the apartment you are considering and for every comp that has a concession, so you compare like with like. The real monthly cost guide walks through the math with required fees added too.

4. Find the median and count

Sort your comps by effective rent and take the median, the middle one. Averages get pulled around by one luxury unit or one mispriced ad; the median holds. Then count: how many comps are asking more than this apartment, and how many are asking less? “Higher than 7 of 9 similar 2-beds nearby” is a clearer statement than any adjective.

When sizes vary, rent per square foot is a useful tiebreaker: a 900 sq ft unit at $2,400 ($2.67 per sq ft) is a different deal from a 1,150 sq ft unit at $2,500 ($2.17).

5. Read where it lands

  • Near the median: the rent is typical for that kind of unit nearby right now. Decide on the apartment itself.
  • Above most comps: something should explain it, like a renovated kitchen, included parking, a larger floor plan or a better floor. If nothing does, the listing has probably been sitting. Check the days listed and any rent cut in the price history; a unit that has been up for 45 days at the same ask is telling you the ask is high. That is your opening to offer less or ask for a concession.
  • Far below every comp: a rent that is 40% or more under similar listings is not usually a bargain. Treat it as a reason to verify the listing, the property and the person collecting money before you send anything. The rental scam guide covers what to check.

Keep the comparison to listing facts: bedrooms, bathrooms, size, building, what is included, days listed and concessions. That is what sets rent, it is what you can verify, and it is what a landlord will respond to when you negotiate.

What to do with the answer

If the ask is above the comps, say so with numbers: “Similar 2-beds within half a mile are asking $2,300 to $2,450; would you do $2,400, or match the six weeks free down the street?” A unit that has been listed a while gives you more room than one posted yesterday. Rent increase rules and renewal rights vary by place; check your state or city’s tenant resources for those.
