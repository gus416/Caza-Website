Is a 6% cap rate good when mortgage rates are 7%?

Why a 6% cap rate struggles against a loan at about 7%: the loan constant, positive vs negative leverage, a worked example, and what changes the result.

Investor guide5 min readUpdated November 2026

Short answer: Usually not on its own. A 30-year loan at 7% costs about 7.98% of the balance each year, which is more than a 6% cap rate earns, so borrowing hurts your return and cash flow lands near zero. Leverage helps only when the cap rate is above the loan constant.

What is the loan constant, and why does it decide this?

The loan constant is annual debt service divided by the loan amount. It is the yearly cost of each dollar borrowed, principal and interest together. A 30-year loan at 7% has a constant of about 7.98%. An interest-only loan at 7% has a constant of exactly 7.0%.

The cap rate is NOI divided by price, so it is what each dollar of property earns before the loan. Compare the two:

  • Cap rate above the loan constant: positive leverage. Each borrowed dollar earns more than it costs, so borrowing raises your cash-on-cash return.
  • Cap rate below the loan constant: negative leverage. Each borrowed dollar costs more than it earns, so borrowing lowers your return.

Mortgage rates around 7% put the amortizing constant near 8%, which is above many cap rates. That is why a 6% cap rate feels thin right now. These are illustrative numbers, not forecasts.

Is a 6% cap rate good with a 7% mortgage?

Here is the plain math on a sample property bought with a 75% loan. Closing costs are ignored to keep it readable, and the numbers use the unrounded payment.

Price$500,000
Cap rate and NOI6.0%, so NOI is $30,000
Loan75% of price, $375,000 at 7%, 30 years
Monthly principal and interest$2,495

DSCR (NOI ÷ debt service)1.00
Annual cash flow$61
Cash invested$125,000 down payment
Cash-on-cash return0.05%

The property earns 6.0% on its price, so an all-cash buyer would see a 6.0% cash-on-cash return. The 75% loan cuts that to 0.05%, because the debt costs 7.98% to earn 6.0%. NOI covers the payment and nothing more. Any vacancy, repair or rate surprise pushes cash flow negative.

What changes the answer?

Four levers move the result. Each row below changes one thing from the example above.

  • More down payment turns cash flow positive and lifts DSCR, but the return stays under 6%, because the loan constant is still above the cap rate. You are lowering the damage, not creating positive leverage.
  • A lower rate shrinks the constant. At 6.5% it is 7.58%, still above 6%.
  • Interest-only drops the constant to the rate itself, but you owe the full balance later, and the constant is still above 6%.
  • Rent upside is the lever that changes the cap rate. A 10% higher NOI is a 6.6% cap rate on the same price. It helps, but it is a bet on rent you have not collected yet.

Lenders also look at DSCR, and many want more than 1.0, often 1.2 or higher. Minimums vary by lender and loan type.

How do you check this on a Zillow listing?

  1. Estimate rent from nearby active rentals. See how to estimate rent with rental comps.
  2. Subtract vacancy, taxes after reassessment, insurance and repairs to get NOI, then divide by price for the cap rate. Steps are in how to check cap rate and cash flow.
  3. Compute the loan constant for your rate and term: annual payment divided by loan amount.
  4. Compare. If the cap rate is below the constant, expect thin or negative cash flow unless you add equity or find rent upside.

The 1% rule is a faster first screen, but it cannot tell you whether the loan helps or hurts.

FAQ

Is a 6% cap rate good with a 7% mortgage?

Not by itself. A 30-year loan at 7% has a loan constant of about 7.98%, above a 6% cap rate, so the loan lowers your return. On a $500,000 property with 75% financing, cash flow comes out near zero.

What is positive leverage in real estate?

Positive leverage means the cap rate is higher than the loan constant, so each borrowed dollar earns more than it costs. When the cap rate is lower, leverage is negative.

How do you calculate the loan constant?

Divide annual debt service by the loan amount. A $375,000 loan at 7% over 30 years costs about $29,939 a year, so the constant is about 7.98%. An interest-only loan at 7% has a constant of 7.0%.
