How to tell if a Zillow listing is overpriced

Overpriced is not the same as expensive. Five checks on the listing page, from matched sales to the rent, that show whether the asking price is ahead of what buyers have paid.

Buyer guide6 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: Compare the asking price with recent nearby sales matched on condition, features, lot and timing. Then use the price history to see how long the current selling attempt has run, check whether saves are low for that time, and run a quick rent check. If several point the same way, dig deeper before you offer.

What does “overpriced” mean on a listing?

Overpriced is not the same as expensive. A $900,000 house can be priced in line with what similar homes sold for, and a $300,000 house can be priced well above them. The question is whether recent buyers paid this much for homes like this one.

The listing page has most of what you need. None of these signals proves what a seller will accept. They tell you where to look; the comps tell you what buyers actually paid.

Check 1: How does the price compare with matched sales?

Start with homes that have sold, not homes that are listed.

Pull sales from roughly the last three to six months, close by, of the same property type and similar size. Then match harder:

  • Condition. A fully remodeled kitchen and an original one are not the same comp.
  • Features. Garage, pool, extra bathroom, finished basement.
  • Lot. A double lot or a busy-street lot can move the price a lot.
  • Timing. A sale from before a big rate move may not reflect today.

Where sale prices aren’t public record, sold prices may be missing; ask an agent. Drop the comps that don’t match instead of averaging them in. Three close matches tell you more than ten loose ones. Price per square foot helps only after the matching is done. Our guide on how to pull comps on Zillow walks through this step by step.

Check 2: How long has the current selling attempt really run?

“Days on Zillow” is a starting point, not the full story. It can start over when a home is taken off the market and listed again, depending on how the listing reaches Zillow. So rebuild the timeline yourself from the price history section:

  1. Find the earliest “Listed for sale” entry in the recent run.
  2. Note any “Listing removed” and relist entries after it. A short break before a relist usually belongs to the same attempt.
  3. Count the days from that first listing to today.

That number is the current selling attempt. A home showing 16 days on Zillow may have been trying to sell for three months. Compare that with how long similar homes nearby took to go under contract. More on this in what days on market means on Zillow.

Check 3: What do the price cuts say?

Price history also shows every cut. A pattern of several small cuts over a long attempt often means the seller is still searching for a price buyers will accept. One early cut can simply move the home into a lower search bracket.

A cut tells you the first number didn’t work, not where the final number will land. See what a price cut on a Zillow listing tells you for the different patterns.

Check 4: Are buyers saving it?

Saves (the heart button) and views show how much attention a listing is getting. What matters is attention relative to time listed. A home with few saves after a long selling attempt is a signal that buyers may be passing on the price. A home with plenty of saves and still no deal may have a problem the price doesn’t explain, like condition or layout.

Compare against similar listings nearby at the same time, not against a fixed number.

Check 5: What does the rent say?

For one more angle, look up asking rents for similar homes nearby. Divide the monthly rent by the price. The “1% rule” (monthly rent at least 1% of the price) is a rough screen, not a test of whether a price is fair. A very low ratio doesn’t make a home overpriced for someone who plans to live there. It does tell you the price isn’t being supported by rental demand, which matters if you plan to rent it out.

A worked example

Example scenario · sample data, not a real listing.

A 3 bed, 2 bath, 1,640 sq ft single-family home is listed at $489,000 ($298 per sq ft).

The three matched sales run $455,000 to $472,000, with a middle sale of $458,000. The asking price sits about $31,000 above that, roughly 7% ($298 per sq ft against $278 to $290). D and E look like support for the price, but each has something this home doesn’t.

Price history: listed July 21 at $519,000, cut to $499,000 August 20, removed September 15, listed again October 12 at $489,000. As of October 28, Days on Zillow shows 16. The current selling attempt has run 99 days.

Demand: 22 saves. Similar listings nearby that went under contract this season drew noticeably more saves in less time.

Rent check: similar homes ask about $2,600 a month, or 0.53% of the price. Not an issue if you plan to live there; thin if you plan to rent it out.

Reading it together: above the matched sales, a long selling attempt behind a short Days on Zillow count, a cut plus a lower relist price, and modest saves. That adds up to a price that looks stretched. It’s a reason to base an offer on sales A through C and ask the agent about the gap. It doesn’t tell you what the seller will take, and none of this is an appraisal.

What should you do with the answer?

  • Buyers: build your offer from matched sales, not from the asking price or the cuts. Ask the listing agent which comps support the price; their answer is useful either way.
  • Listing agents: the same five checks make a clear pricing talk with a seller, built on sales and buyer attention.
  • Investors: if the rent check is thin, run cap rate and cash flow with your own financing before you go further.
