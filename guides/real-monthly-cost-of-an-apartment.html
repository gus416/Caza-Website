The real monthly cost of an apartment: fees, utilities and “weeks free”

The advertised rent is not what you pay. How to add required monthly fees (parking, pet rent, trash, amenity, utilities), turn "6 weeks free" into effective rent, and a worked example with sample numbers.

For renters5 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: the real monthly cost of an apartment is the rent, plus every fee you are required to pay each month, plus the utilities you pay on your own. If the listing offers free weeks or months, the effective rent is rent × (lease months − free months) ÷ lease months. Work both numbers out before you compare two apartments, because a lower sticker with $250 in fees can cost more than a higher sticker with none.

Listings lead with one number; leases have several. Here is how to get from the advertised rent to what actually leaves your account each month.

Required monthly fees to look for

These are the recurring charges that show up on the lease or the move-in sheet. Some listings say whether the advertised price includes them; many do not, so ask before you apply.

  • Parking. A space, garage or permit. In many buildings this is $50 to $300 a month and is not in the rent.
  • Pet rent. A monthly charge per pet, separate from any one-time pet deposit or pet fee.
  • Trash and valet trash. A flat monthly fee for pickup, sometimes mandatory even if you carry your own bags out.
  • Amenity, community or technology package. Gym, pool, package lockers, bundled internet or cable. Often required whether you use them or not.
  • Utility billing. Water, sewer and gas billed back by the building, sometimes as a share of the building’s total (often called RUBS), plus a monthly billing fee for the service that sends the bill.
  • Renters insurance. Many leases require a policy. It is cheap, but it is monthly, so include it.
  • Utilities you pay directly. Electricity, internet, sometimes gas. Ask the leasing office what a typical month runs for that unit, or ask the current tenant at the tour.

Keep one-time costs (application fee, admin fee, deposits, move-in fee) in a separate column. They decide the cash you need on day one, not the monthly number.

How to read “6 weeks free”

A concession is a discount spread over the lease. To see what it does to your monthly cost, spread it yourself:

  • Free months: effective rent = rent × (lease months − free months) ÷ lease months. One month free on a 12-month lease at $2,000 is $2,000 × 11 ÷ 12 = $1,833 a month.
  • Free weeks: convert weeks to months first. Six weeks is 6 × 12 ÷ 52, about 1.4 months. Eight weeks is about 1.8.
  • “$X off”: divide by the lease length. $600 off a 12-month lease is $50 a month.
  • “Up to”: means conditions apply, usually a longer lease, a specific floor plan or a move-in by a date. Ask which units and terms actually get the full offer.

Three things to remember. The concession usually lives in the first lease only; at renewal the rent goes back to the full sticker, so the sticker still matters. Many leases take the free weeks up front, so month one is cheap and every other month is full rent; budget on the full number. And if the lease is 13 or 14 months, use that length in the math.

A worked example (sample numbers)

These are example figures to show the math, not a real listing.

Example only: a 2-bed advertised at $2,450 a month with “6 weeks free” on a 12-month lease
LinePer month
Advertised rent$2,450
Required parking (one space)$100
Required trash fee$25
All-in monthly cost (rent plus required fees)$2,575
Effective rent after 6 weeks free: $2,450 × (12 − 1.385) ÷ 12$2,167
Effective all-in cost over the first year$2,292
Your own electricity and internet (estimate from the leasing office)$140
What leaves your account, averaged over 12 months$2,432

Now put a second apartment next to it. Say a 2-bed at $2,300 with no concession, parking included, a $30 trash fee and similar utilities. Its all-in cost is $2,330, and with no free weeks that is also its effective cost; add $140 of utilities and it is $2,470. The first apartment advertises a higher rent and ends up about $40 a month cheaper in year one, and then about $250 more expensive in year two if the concession does not repeat. Which one is better depends on how long you plan to stay, and now you can see that.

Two numbers to carry into any comparison: the all-in monthly cost (rent plus required fees) and the effective rent (after concessions). The first is what you budget for; the second is what the first year actually costs.

Questions to ask before you apply

  1. Does the advertised rent include required monthly fees? If not, what are they, and which are optional?
  2. Is the concession on this exact unit and lease length, and is it spread across the lease or taken in month one?
  3. What does a typical month of utilities run for this floor plan?
  4. What are the one-time fees and deposits, and which are refundable?
  5. What happens to the rent at renewal? Rules on increases and notice vary by place, so check your state or city’s tenant resources.
