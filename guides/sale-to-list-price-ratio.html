Sale-to-list price ratio: how to read it before an offer

What the ratio measures, which list price it uses, what it hides, and why a market-wide number isn’t a discount you can apply to the house you want.

For buyers and buyer’s agents7 min readLast updated November 26, 2026

Short answer

The sale-to-list ratio compares what homes sold for with what they were listed at. Read the publisher’s definition first: Redfin’s Average Sale to List Ratio uses each home’s final list price, so a home that was cut before it sold can still show 100%.1 And a market-wide ratio describes many different homes. It isn’t a discount to take off one listing’s asking price. Price that home from recent nearby sales of similar homes.

Before you lean on it for an offer:

  1. Check which list price it uses. Final and original list price give different answers after a price cut.
  2. Remember what it leaves out. Seller credits, condition and the mix of homes that sold.
  3. Price the house, not the market. Use comparable sales and the home’s own price history.

1. What is the sale-to-list price ratio?

For one home, it’s the sale price divided by the list price. A result above 1 (or 100%) means it sold above list; below 1 means it sold below list.

For a whole market, a publisher combines many of those home-by-home ratios into one figure. Redfin’s Data Center defines its Average Sale to List Ratio as: “For homes sold during a given period, the average ratio of each home’s final sale price to its final list price.”1

Two details in that definition matter:

  • It’s an average of ratios. Redfin works out the ratio for each sold home, then averages them. That isn’t the same as dividing a median sale price by a median list price, so don’t try to rebuild it from those headline numbers.
  • Some sales are left out. Redfin says: “Sales where the ratio is less than 0.5 or greater than 2.0 are excluded.”1

Other publishers may choose differently. Before you compare two sites’ numbers, read each one’s methodology page.

2. Final list price or original list price: which one is it?

It depends on the publisher, and it matters for any home that had a price cut.

Redfin publishes both. Next to that ratio, it defines an Average Sale to Original List Ratio: “For homes sold during a given period, the average ratio of each home’s final sale price to its original list price.” It also splits “sold above list” the same way: Share Sold Above List uses “the most recent list price,” and Share Sold Above Original List uses “the original list price.”1

The final-list version shows how close sales came to the last asking price. The original-list version also captures the cuts on the way there.

A worked example (hypothetical)

This home and its numbers are made up to show the arithmetic. They aren’t typical, and they aren’t data from any real listing or market.

Hypothetical example: one home with a price cut
StepAmount
Original list price$500,000
Price cut−$40,000
Final list price$460,000
Sale price$460,000
Seller credit toward the buyer’s closing costs$9,200
  • Sale to final list: $460,000 ÷ $460,000 = 1.00, or 100%
  • Sale to original list: $460,000 ÷ $500,000 = 0.92, or 92%
  • Sale price minus the credit, to final list: $450,800 ÷ $460,000 = 0.98, or 98%

The same sale reads “sold at list” or “sold 8% under original list,” depending on which list price you use. And neither ratio shows the $9,200 credit. The last line is only a way to see the size of the credit. It isn’t how an appraiser would treat it.

3. What does the sale-to-list ratio hide?

A ratio is built from two prices. Anything that isn’t in those two prices doesn’t show up.

  • Seller concessions and credits. The definitions above compare the sale price with the list price and don’t mention credits. Fannie Mae’s examples of sales or financing concessions include “interest rate buydowns or other below-market rate financing,” “loan discount points” and “closing costs customarily paid by the buyer.”2 For appraisals on loans it buys, Fannie Mae says a comparable sale with concessions “must be adjusted to reflect the impact, if any,” on its sales price.2 The ratio makes no such adjustment.
  • Condition and features. A renovated home that sold over list and a fixer that sold under list go into the same average, with no hint of why.
  • How the list price was set. A home listed low may get a high ratio; one listed high and never cut may get a low one. The ratio grades the asking price as much as the house.
  • The mix of homes. A market-wide figure blends whatever sold in that period: different prices, sizes, home types and neighborhoods. If the mix shifts, the ratio can move even when nothing changed for homes like yours.
  • Homes that didn’t sell. Withdrawn and still-listed homes aren’t in it.

4. Why isn’t a market ratio a discount for one house?

Because it answers a different question. A market ratio describes how sale prices compared with asking prices across many homes. Your offer depends on what this one home is worth, and its list price may be high, low or about right.

Say you offer the area’s ratio times the asking price. If the seller listed well above what similar homes sold for, your number can still be too high. If they listed below, you may offer less than the home would fetch. Either way, the ratio told you nothing about the house.

Appraisals work from the house up. The CFPB says “Appraisal values are generally determined by making adjustments between the features of your home and comparable local property sales (which are called ‘comps’ for short).”3 You can follow the same order:

  1. Pull recent nearby sales of similar homes. Same property type, similar size, beds and baths, close by and recent. See how to pull comps on Zillow.
  2. Read each comp’s own history. Where the price history shows it, note the original list price, any cuts and the sale price. A comp that sold “at list” after two cuts tells a different story from one that sold at its first price.
  3. Ask about concessions. A sale price alone doesn’t say whether the seller paid part of the buyer’s costs. Your agent or the listing agent may know.
  4. Compare the asking price with the comps. See how to tell if a Zillow listing is overpriced.
  5. Then read this listing’s history. A cut on the home you want is its own signal. See what a price cut on a Zillow listing tells you.

Treat the market ratio as background on how sale prices have compared with asking prices, at most. Use the comps for the number you write.

What this guide can’t tell you

  • What a specific seller will accept, or whether there are other offers
  • The current sale-to-list ratio for your area (this guide quotes no market data)
  • How publishers other than Redfin define or calculate their ratios
  • What concessions came with a particular sale, unless someone involved tells you
  • What an appraisal will come in at, or the home’s condition (that takes an inspection)

FAQ

Does “100% of list” mean a home sold for what the seller first asked?

Not necessarily. Redfin’s Average Sale to List Ratio compares the sale price with the final list price. A home that was cut and then sold at the new price shows 100% of final list, even though it sold below its original list price. Check the price history.1

Can I apply my area’s sale-to-list ratio to a listing’s asking price?

No. A market-wide ratio averages many different homes, and it measures how sale prices compared with asking prices, not what any one home is worth. Price the home you want from recent nearby sales of similar homes.

Does the sale-to-list ratio include seller credits?

The definitions we read compare the sale price with the list price and say nothing about credits. A seller who paid part of the buyer’s closing costs can still show a high ratio. Ask what concessions came with each sale you use as a comp.

Why do two sites show different sale-to-list ratios for the same area?

They may measure different things: final or original list price, an average or a median, which sales they leave out, which home types, and which period. Redfin alone publishes two versions. Read each publisher’s methodology before comparing.1

Does Caza calculate a sale-to-list ratio?

No. For a house, Caza’s Sales comps report shows how the listing’s price per square foot compares with the comps’ median sold price per square foot, as a percent above or below. That’s a comparison with nearby sales, not a sale-to-list ratio.4
