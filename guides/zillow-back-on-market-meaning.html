What does “back on market” mean on a Zillow listing?

What can happen before a listing comes back, what the listing can and can’t tell you, and the questions to ask before you write an offer.

For buyers and buyer’s agents9 min readLast updated November 13, 2026

Short answer

Back on market means a home that had stopped being available is for sale again. One way it happens: the seller accepted an offer, the home went pending or contingent, and the contract ended before closing. The label doesn’t say why. Read the price history and ask the listing agent before you write an offer.

Two more things to know:

  1. Status names and cumulative-days rules are set by each local MLS, and they differ. The day count Zillow shows is Zillow’s own and may not match the MLS’s.2345
  2. Back on market isn’t automatically good or bad. It’s a reason to look closer.

1. What happened before a listing came back on market?

The basic path of a sale is: listed → offer accepted → under contract → closed. A contract can include contingencies, such as a satisfactory appraisal or inspection.1 A listing comes back when that chain breaks after an offer was accepted, or when a home that was taken off the market is listed again.3

On the MLS side, the in-between statuses vary. In status rules that a Texas Realtor association (MetroTex) published for its MLS (file dated May 2020), for example:2

  • Active Contingent: “Seller has accepted an offer but has requested that the property remain available for showings and Seller will entertain backup offers.”
  • Pending: the property has an offer with no contingencies, kick-outs or options, and “the seller requests no further showings and does not want to entertain backup offers.”

A St. Louis–area MLS describes “Pending” as an accepted contract where the seller has told the agent “to cease marketing and/or showing the property, while normal contractual conditions are fulfilled (e.g., inspections, financing, etc.)”3 When a contract like that ends, the agent can return the listing to “Active.”3 The MetroTex document’s list of flags for the agent-facing search results screen includes a “B” next to the MLS number, defined only as “Indicates the listing is back on market,” and it says those flags “will appear for 5 days.”2 Other MLSs may do it differently.

We couldn’t confirm from Zillow’s own help pages how it defines “back on market” or where the label appears, so this guide doesn’t describe Zillow’s wording. Here is what we can say. Zillow’s listing data includes a status text, and the Caza extension looks for “back on market” in that text to set its back-on-market flag.7 You don’t have to rely on the label at all: the price history on the listing can show when a home went pending and when it was listed again (section 3).6

2. Why do deals fall through?

There’s no single reason, and the Zillow label doesn’t say which one it was. This guide doesn’t rank them or say how often each happens.

A buyer’s offer can be conditional. A sample buyer’s contingency published by the Minnesota Attorney General’s Office makes the purchase depend on a “satisfactory appraisal valuing the property at an amount not less than the purchase price” and a home inspection report that the house is “structurally sound and in good repair.” If a contingency isn’t satisfied within the time the form provides, the form lets the agreement “become null and void at the option of and upon written notice by Buyer,” with earnest money refunded.1 Real contracts vary by state and by the forms used.

So the possibilities include:

  • Inspection. The inspection report didn’t satisfy the buyer’s contingency, and the buyer chose to end the contract.1
  • Appraisal. The appraisal came in under the purchase price, and the deal didn’t go ahead at that price.1
  • The buyer’s loan. The financing didn’t come through. One MLS lists financing, alongside inspections, among the “normal contractual conditions” that are fulfilled while a home is pending.3
  • The buyer’s other home. The offer depended on the buyer selling a home they already own. The MetroTex document has a separate status for an offer “contingent upon the sale of another property by buyer.”2
  • Something else in the contract. The sample Minnesota form leaves blank lines for other conditions the parties add.1

Only some of these say anything about the house. You can’t tell which one it was from the listing alone.

3. How do you read the price history?

Open Price history on the Zillow listing page. It’s the best public record of what happened, and it’s the same place the days on market guide uses to rebuild a selling attempt: find when the current attempt began and note any pending stretches since then.6

Look for:

  • When it went pending (or contingent) and when it came back. The time in between is how long the deal lasted. The listing doesn’t say why it ended, and contract deadlines (for example, the date an inspection report is due in the sample form above1) differ from deal to deal. Use the length as a prompt for questions, not a conclusion.
  • Whether it has happened more than once. A home that has gone under contract and come back several times is worth a direct question: what keeps ending the deals?
  • Price changes around the relist. A cut right after coming back may mean the seller learned something from an appraisal, an inspection, or the market. See what a price cut on a Zillow listing tells you.
  • The days-on-market number. Days on Zillow can reset when a home is relisted, depending on the listing source and how it was relisted.6 MLS rules for cumulative days on market differ too. One MLS sums days across listings “when there are 61 days or less between the end of one listing and the start of another.”4 Another counts a “substantial break” as 31 days or longer in cancelled, expired or sold status, and says its own day counts “are not shared with 3rd party sites such as REALTOR.com or Zillow.”2 A third requires a listing to stay off market “a FULL 30 calendar days” and come back “as a NEW listing” before cumulative days reset, and says its day counts “do not accrue while the listing is in ‘Pending’ status.”3 The industry data standard defines cumulative days on market “as defined by the MLS business rules.”5 Trust the price history over the counter, and ask your agent for the MLS’s cumulative days on market.

4. When is back on market an opportunity, and when should you be careful?

Back on market isn’t automatically good or bad. It’s a reason to look closer, then ask the listing agent why the last contract ended.

Reasons it can work in your favor

  • The seller already expected to sell, and may be ready to move faster.
  • If the earlier buyer’s reason had nothing to do with the house, you get a second chance at a home you might have missed.
  • If an inspection already happened, the seller may know about issues and may be willing to share or address them. Ask. Still get your own inspection.

Reasons to be careful

  • The earlier buyer may have found a real problem. If a seller knows about defects, disclosure rules in your state may apply. Ask your agent what the seller has to disclose. This guide can’t tell you.
  • If the last deal ended over the appraisal, your lender’s appraisal could come in low too.
  • Repeated fall-throughs are a pattern to ask about.

Questions to ask the listing agent (through your agent, if you have one)

  1. Why did the last contract end?
  2. Was an inspection done? Will the seller share the report, or any repair estimates?
  3. Did an appraisal come in? Was it below the contract price?
  4. Has the price or any term changed since the relist?
  5. Is the seller open to a quick close, and what terms matter most to them?

What this guide can’t tell you

  • Why a specific deal fell through. Only the parties and their agents know, and they may not say.
  • How often deals fall through in your market. This guide doesn’t cite a rate.
  • Your local MLS’s status names, or how it counts days on market after a relist.
  • Exactly how and where Zillow labels a returned listing. We couldn’t confirm it from Zillow’s own pages.
  • What a seller is legally required to disclose in your state.
  • Whether a particular home is a good deal.

FAQ

Does back on market mean something is wrong with the house?

Not necessarily. Deals can end for reasons on the buyer’s side, like a loan or a home the buyer needed to sell. Ask why, and get your own inspection.23

Is back on market the same as a new listing?

No. It’s the same home returning after a deal ended or after time off the market. The price history can show the earlier pending period. Depending on MLS rules, the days-on-market count may or may not carry over.234

Can I offer less on a back-on-market home?

You can offer what you think it’s worth. Base it on recent sales, not the label. “How to spot a Zillow listing that will take a low offer” covers the other signals.

What’s the difference between contingent and pending?

It depends on the MLS. In one, “contingent” means an offer is accepted but the home is still shown and backup offers are taken, while “pending” means no more showings or backup offers.2 Ask your agent how your local MLS uses them.

Does the days-on-market count reset when a home comes back?

It depends on the MLS and the listing source. One MLS carries days over unless there’s a gap of more than 61 days, another unless there’s a break of 31 days or longer, and a third requires 30 full days off market and a new listing. If the same listing is returned to active after a contract ends, one MLS says its count picks up from the last day on market; the reset rules apply when a home is relisted after time off the market. Trust the price history over the counter, and ask your agent for the local cumulative-days rule.2345
