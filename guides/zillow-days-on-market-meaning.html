What does “days on market” actually mean on Zillow?

“Days on Zillow” counts time on Zillow, not time for sale. What resets the clock, how to find the real number in the price history, and how to negotiate.

Buyer guide4 min readUpdated October 2026

Short answer: “Days on Zillow” is how many days the current listing has been active on Zillow. Depending on the listing source and how the home was relisted, it can reset, so time on market can be longer than the counter shows. The price history on the listing page lets you rebuild the current selling attempt.

The number labeled “Days on Zillow” is the most misread metric on the site. Buyers think it tells them how long the home has been for sale. It does, with caveats that change the whole interpretation.

The official definition

Zillow’s “Days on Zillow” (often shortened to DOM, for days on market) counts the days the listing has been active on Zillow specifically. This is not the same as how long the home has been for sale.

Listings reach Zillow through MLS feeds. If an agent lists on the MLS today, it shows up on Zillow within hours and the clock starts. If the same home was for sale months ago, taken off the market for a while, and re-listed yesterday under a new listing, the counter can start over. The rules depend on the listing source: for MLS-fed listings it generally follows the MLS on-market date, and a quick relist by the same agent doesn’t always reset it. To you, “Days on Zillow: 1” reads as a fresh listing. In reality, it could be a stale home with a new MLS entry.

What resets the counter

  • Re-listing. Pulling the home off the market and re-listing it can reset the clock, especially after a longer break or under a new listing number. It doesn’t always: the rules vary by listing source and local MLS.
  • Switching listing agents. If the seller changes brokerages, the new MLS entry typically resets the counter on Zillow.
  • Status changes. Going from “Pending” back to “Active” when a deal falls through sometimes resets the visible DOM, sometimes doesn’t.

The fix: open Price history on the listing page. If you see a date six months ago marked “Listed for sale” followed by “Listing removed,” the home has been on the market longer than the counter shows. The price history is much harder to game than the DOM.

Why DOM matters for negotiation

Real DOM, the actual time a home has been trying to sell, is one of the strongest negotiation signals you have as a buyer. A home that has been sitting for months is more likely to take a lower price, and a seller who won’t move on price will often move on terms: closing costs, repairs, contingencies.

Conversely, a home that has been on the market under 7 days with a high save count is one where you write your strongest offer or move on. Trying to negotiate a fresh, busy listing wastes everyone’s time and can cost you the home.

How to read DOM correctly

  1. Check “Days on Zillow” for the surface number.
  2. Open “Price history” and find when the current selling attempt began. Skip anything before the last sale, and note withdrawn or pending stretches. The time since that start is the best public estimate; your agent can confirm the official DOM and cumulative DOM in the MLS.
  3. Combine that with the save count. A home 90 days into its current selling attempt with 8 saves is sitting. One at 90 days with 200 saves drew plenty of interest and still didn’t sell, which often points to price or condition.
